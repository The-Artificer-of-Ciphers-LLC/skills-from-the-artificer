## 2. (a) Why explaining surfaces bugs; LLM-as-duck

- Illusion of explanatory depth. Rozenblit & Keil, Cognitive Science 26(5):521-562, 2002 (metadata and abstract via Crossref, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1): people think they understand complex phenomena much better than they do; the illusion is much stronger for explanatory (mechanism) knowledge than for facts, procedures or narratives. Implication: "I understand this code path" is exactly the confidence that forced step-by-step explanation deflates. The abstract does not state the "rate your understanding, then explain, then re-rate" procedure; I do not cite it.
- Self-explanation effect. Chi et al. 1989, "Self-explanations: How students study and use examples in learning to solve problems", Cognitive Science 13(2):145-182: title/venue confirmed via Crossref (https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/0364-0213(89)90002-5). UNVERIFIED: the abstract/findings (publisher pages returned 403, Crossref has no abstract). Not used for any claim here. Same for Chi et al. 1994.
- LLM as its own duck, with execution grounding. Chen et al., "Teaching Large Language Models to Self-Debug", https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05128: the model can "perform rubber duck debugging" without human feedback on correctness by "investigating the execution results and explaining the generated code in natural language". Reported gains: code explanation alone improves the baseline 2-3% on Spider (no unit tests); up to 12% on TransCoder/MBPP where unit tests exist; 9% on the hardest Spider level. Reading: explanation helps a little alone, more when paired with test/execution signal.
- Listener does not need to be smart. Robot Duck Debugging, https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06511 (101 participants): neither a rule-based nor a deep-learning robot listener improved performance or perception of the task compared with an oversized rubber duck. Implication: the value is in the speaker's articulation, so an agent-duck's added value must come from something a duck cannot do (reading code, running things), not from commentary.
- Other "rubber duck" LLM papers (Digital Rubber Duck, debug-gym's rubber duck tool, EHRAgent) were seen only in search snippets and are not cited.

## 3. (b) Autonomous self-interrogation with no human

- Self-ask. Press et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03350: the model "explicitly asks itself (and answers) follow-up questions before answering the initial question"; motivated by a measured "compositionality gap" (answers every sub-problem but misses the composed answer). Supports decomposing a claim/design into sub-questions.
- Chain-of-Verification. Dhuliawala et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11495: draft, plan verification questions, answer them separately so the draft does not bias answers, then write the final response. Full text (via ar5iv, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.11495): joint verification conditions on the draft and can repeat its errors; factored (each question in its own prompt, draft omitted) was better on every task. Llama-65B list precision, Wikidata: baseline 0.17, joint 0.29, factored 0.32; FactScore on biographies 60.8 joint vs 63.7 factored. Note: CoVe's verification questions were answered by the model itself (no tools) and it reduced, not eliminated, hallucination. Implication: answer each audit question from evidence without re-reading your own prior answer first.
- Self-consistency. Wang et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171: sample several reasoning chains, take the majority answer; gains over CoT of +17.9% GSM8K, +11.0% SVAMP, +12.2% AQuA, +6.4% StrategyQA, +3.9% ARC-c. Applicable to reasoning with a checkable final answer, not to open-ended "is this design sound".
- Reflexion. Shinn et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366: verbal reflections on task feedback stored in memory, no weight updates; feedback may be scalar or free-form from external or internally simulated sources; 91% pass@1 on HumanEval vs GPT-4's 80% as reported. The abstract does not say which feedback source drives the gain; in coding setups the signal is test results (I did not confirm this from the paper text, so treat as unverified).
- Where self-critique fails. Huang et al., "LLMs Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet", https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798 (full text via https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01798): "LLMs struggle to self-correct their responses without external feedback", sometimes getting worse. Section 3.2: earlier gains relied on oracle ground-truth labels to decide when to stop and "can only be regarded as indicative of an oracle's performance"; without labels self-correction lowered accuracy. Section 4: multi-agent debate is no better than self-consistency at equal response count (GSM8K, 9 responses: 83.0 vs 88.2). Section 6: valid external feedback (code execution, unit tests, calculators, search) helps. Scope caveat: the paper studies reasoning tasks (math, QA), not code review.
- Grounding fixes it. CRITIC, Gou et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738: the model "interacts with appropriate tools to evaluate certain aspects of the text" then revises, stressing "the crucial importance of external feedback". Chen et al. above (execution results) and Olausson et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896: self-repair gains are "often modest" once cost is counted, and "bottlenecked by the model's ability to provide feedback on its own code"; stronger-model or human feedback helped considerably more. Implication: an agent that interrogates itself with no human must make each answer an observation (a grep hit, a file:line, a test result), not another opinion.

## 4. (c) Verifying PR/design claims against code

- Google eng-practices, https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/looking-for.html: reviewers check design ("the most important thing to cover in a review is the overall design"), functionality ("Does this CL do what the developer intended?", edge cases, concurrency), tests ("Will the tests actually fail when the code is broken?"), comments that "explain why". This frames a claim audit as checking stated intent against behavior, and tests as evidence only if they would fail on breakage.
- Bird & Bacchelli (ICSE 2013, "Expectations, Outcomes, and Challenges of Modern Code Review"), https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/expectations-outcomes-and-challenges-of-modern-code-review/: reviews find fewer defects than expected; "code and change understanding is the key aspect of code reviewing". Supports leading with "what does this change actually do" before judging.
- I found no primary source prescribing a "claim extraction then per-claim evidence check" procedure for PR descriptions. That workflow is an inference from CoVe (plan questions, answer independently, section 3) and Self-ask (decompose), not a documented review practice. Treat it as a design choice and say so.

## 5. Anthropic skill-authoring guidance

Source: https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/best-practices (fetched in full).
- Concise: "Only add context Claude doesn't already have"; test each paragraph against its token cost.
- Description is the trigger surface: must say what the skill does and when to use it, in third person; Claude picks among potentially 100+ skills by it; "Avoid vague descriptions".
- Progressive disclosure: keep SKILL.md body under 500 lines; split into files; references one level deep.
- Degrees of freedom: high freedom where "multiple approaches are valid / decisions depend on context"; low freedom where operations are fragile or "a specific sequence must be followed".
- Feedback loops: "Run validator, fix errors, repeat" "greatly improves output quality"; copyable checklists for multistep non-code workflows such as claim cross-referencing ("For each major claim, verify it appears in the source material"); "Create verifiable intermediate outputs" (plan, validate, execute).
- Iterate from real usage: observe how agents actually use the skill, then refine (the Observed usage section is that signal).

## 6. Implications for rubber-duck/SKILL.md

1. Add a mode switch near the top: interactive (human present) vs autonomous (no human; args say "autonomous", "walk it", or no reply channel). Keep the current protocol for interactive. Why: observed usage (section 1) is 7/7 autonomous; the agent ignored the protocol, so the protocol is not wrong but is unreachable without a mode.
2. Autonomous mode: replace "ask the human" with "write the question, then answer it from an observation (file:line, command output, test result); mark each answer VERIFIED or ASSUMED". Why: Huang et al. (self-critique without external feedback degrades; valid feedback is code execution/tests/tools), CRITIC, Olausson et al. (feedback quality is the bottleneck).
3. Drop "never look at code yet" and "does not look up APIs" in autonomous mode; invert to "reading code and running a check is the answer mechanism". Why: same findings; Chen et al. gains are larger with execution signal (2-3% explanation only vs up to 12% with unit tests). Keep "expected vs actual in one sentence" as a free, cheap first step (Self-ask decomposition).
4. Add a claim-audit procedure, labeled as a design choice: (a) extract each claim as a numbered one-line checkable statement; (b) for each, answer independently from the code without rereading earlier verdicts; (c) output a table claim / evidence (file:line or command) / verdict (confirmed, refuted, unverifiable). Why: CoVe factored beat joint on every task (precision 0.32 vs 0.29; FactScore 63.7 vs 60.8), Google's "does it do what the developer intended" and "will tests fail when code is broken". Say in the skill that "tests exist" is not evidence unless they would fail on the claimed breakage.
5. Treat "should" and "I think" as a trigger to run a check, not to ask a human. Why: the illusion of explanatory depth is strongest for mechanism knowledge (Rozenblit & Keil); an agent's fluent explanation is not evidence, so the autonomous analogue of the "pause" signal is an unverified mechanism claim.
6. Widen the description's trigger surface to include claim audits and design walks ("walk the claims in a PR description", "walk a design doc against the code", "design for #N, answer your own questions"), in third person, under 1,024 chars. Why: Anthropic's description guidance; observed invocations do not match the current triggers (section 1).
7. Require a final artifact in autonomous mode: the claim/evidence/verdict list plus a one-line list of ASSUMED items left. Why: Anthropic's "verifiable intermediate outputs" and checklists; Huang et al. warn that self-reported confidence alone does not catch errors.
8. Keep the added autonomous section short (aim for under ~40 lines) and leave the interactive protocol untouched; do not split into extra files yet. Why: Anthropic conciseness and 500-line guidance; the file is already well under it.
